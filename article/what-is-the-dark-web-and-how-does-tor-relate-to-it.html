What Is the Dark Web and How Does Tor Relate to It?

The dark web is a hidden layer of the internet that cannot be accessed through standard search engines or conventional browsers, requiring specialized software to access. This article explains the structure of the internet's different layers, defines what the dark web actually is, and explores how The Onion Router (Tor) serves as the primary technology that makes browsing and hosting sites on the dark web possible.

The Three Layers of the Internet

To understand the dark web, it is essential to look at how the internet is divided into three distinct layers:

  1. The Surface Web: This is the visible layer of the internet that most people use daily. It includes indexed websites that standard search engines like Google and Bing can catalog, such as news sites, blogs, and public social media profiles. The surface web represents only a small fraction of the entire internet.
  2. The Deep Web: The deep web consists of unindexed online content that search engines cannot crawl. This includes password-protected pages, private email accounts, online banking portals, medical records, and corporate intranets. The deep web makes up the vast majority of online data and is entirely legal and routine.
  3. The Dark Web: The dark web is a small, intentional subset of the deep web. It consists of websites that are deliberately hidden and encrypted, requiring specialized overlay networks and authorization protocols to access.

What Is the Dark Web?

The dark web is a decentralized network of websites that prioritize complete user and host anonymity. Unlike standard websites that use familiar domain extensions like .com or .org, dark web sites frequently use .onion addresses. These websites are not registered through conventional domain name registrars, meaning they cannot be accessed through standard browsers like Chrome, Safari, or Edge without specific configurations.

While the dark web has a reputation for hosting illicit marketplaces and illegal activities, it also serves critical privacy functions. Journalists, whistleblowers, dissidents in oppressive regimes, and privacy-conscious individuals use the dark web to communicate securely and bypass government censorship without revealing their physical locations or IP addresses.

How Tor Relates to the Dark Web

Tor, short for The Onion Router, is the fundamental technology and network protocol that enables access to the dark web. Developed originally by the U.S. Naval Research Laboratory in the 1990s, Tor is an open-source privacy network designed to protect online communications from surveillance and traffic analysis.

Tor relates to the dark web in two primary ways:

1. The Tor Browser as a Gateway

The standard way users access the dark web is through the Tor Browser. This modified version of Firefox is pre-configured to route all web traffic through the Tor network, allowing users to open .onion links that standard browsers cannot resolve.

2. Onion Routing and Hidden Services

Tor enables the existence of dark web sites through a concept called "onion routing." When a user connects to the dark web via Tor:

  • Traffic is wrapped in multiple layers of encryption (like the layers of an onion).
  • The data is routed through a randomized chain of three volunteer-operated servers, known as nodes (the Guard/Entry node, the Middle node, and the Exit node).
  • Each node only decrypts enough information to know where to send the data packet next, ensuring that no single server knows both the origin IP address and the final destination.

In addition to routing traffic, Tor allows users to set up "Onion Services" (formerly known as Hidden Services). These are websites hosted entirely within the Tor network. Because traffic never leaves the encrypted Tor ecosystem to reach an exit node, both the visitor and the website publisher remain anonymous to each other and to outside observers.
